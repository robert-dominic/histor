## Hook (its own screen, before chapter 1)

The ship was sound.
The cargo was untouched.
There was food for six months.
Everyone on board had vanished.

*December 1872. The Atlantic.*

---

## Chapter 1: The ship and her people

In November 1872, a small cargo ship called the Mary Celeste left New York.
Today, the world remembers her as the ghost ship.
She was heading for Genoa, in Italy, carrying 1,701 barrels of industrial alcohol.

She was not a lucky ship.
She was built in Nova Scotia, Canada, eleven years earlier, under another name: the Amazon.
Her first captain fell ill on her very first voyage, and died.
Years later she was wrecked in a storm, rebuilt, and renamed, in the hope that her luck would change.

Ten people sailed on her that November:
**Benjamin Briggs**, the captain, 37.
**Sarah Briggs**, his wife.
**Sophia Briggs**, their daughter, two years old.
**Albert Richardson**, first mate.
**Andrew Gilling**, second mate.
**Edward Head**, the cook.
**Volkert and Boz Lorenzen**, brothers, sailors.
**Arian Martens** and **Gottlieb Goodschaad**, sailors.

The Briggs had a son, too: Arthur, seven years old.
He stayed at home, so he wouldn't miss school.

That decision saved his life.

---

## Chapter 2: The voyage

On 7 November 1872, the Mary Celeste sailed out of New York.
Before leaving, Captain Briggs wrote to his mother that the ship was "in beautiful trim".

The crossing was rough, with strong winds for days.
But the ship made good time across the Atlantic.

On 25 November, at eight in the morning, someone noted the ship's position.
The island of Santa Maria, in the Azores, was six miles away.

It is the last thing anyone on board ever wrote.

Eight days behind them, another ship had left New York on almost the same route.
The Dei Gratia, under Captain David Morehouse.
Some say he and Briggs were friends, and had dinner together the night before the Mary Celeste sailed.

---

## Chapter 3: The discovery

On 4 December 1872, the crew of the Dei Gratia saw a ship on the horizon.
She was about 400 miles east of the Azores, halfway between the islands and Portugal.
She was moving strangely, drifting off course with her sails in disorder.

Captain Morehouse watched her for two hours.
He signalled. No one answered.

He sent his first mate, Oliver Deveau, across in a small boat.
It was the Mary Celeste.

There was no one on board.

Here is what Deveau found:
The ship's only lifeboat was gone.
There was water in the hold, a lot of it, but not enough to sink her.
There was food and fresh water for six months.
The crew's clothes and belongings were still in their places.
The cargo of alcohol was almost untouched.

But a few things were missing:
the captain's sextant and chronometer, the two instruments a ship needed to know where it was,
and all the ship's papers except the logbook.

The Dei Gratia's men sailed the empty ship to Gibraltar, expecting a reward for saving her.

Instead, they were met with suspicion.

---

## Chapter 4: What doesn't add up

In Gibraltar, a court began to ask questions.
Leading them was Frederick Solly-Flood, the colony's Attorney General.
He was sure there had been a crime: mutiny, murder, or a trick by the men who found her.

The hearings went on for three months.
No proof of any crime was ever found.

The Dei Gratia's crew were paid for saving the ship, but only about a sixth of what she and her cargo were worth.
Many saw it as a sign that the court never fully trusted them.

But the bigger questions remain.

Why would an experienced captain leave a ship that wasn't sinking,
for a small open boat, in the middle of the Atlantic, with his wife and baby daughter?

Why take the instruments and the papers, but leave the food, the water, and everything they owned?

And what happened to the lifeboat?
No trace of it, or of the ten people in it, was ever found.

---

## Chapter 5: The legend grows

The court closed the case. The mystery stayed open.

Twelve years later, a young writer named Arthur Conan Doyle published a story about the ship, without signing his name.
He called her the *Marie* Celeste, and filled the tale with invented details.
Many readers believed it was true.
The wrong name stuck. So did some of the details.

Since then, people have blamed almost everything:
a mutiny, pirates, a sea monster, a waterspout,
a seaquake, poisoned food, fumes from the alcohol in the hold,
and, of course, ghosts.

Scientists have tested theories in laboratories.
Writers have filled books, and filmmakers have made films.
In Britain, her name even became a word: an empty place, abandoned without explanation, is called "a Mary Celeste".

For 150 years, people have argued.
No one has proved what happened.
